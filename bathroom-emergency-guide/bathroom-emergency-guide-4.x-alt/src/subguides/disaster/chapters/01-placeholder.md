# Natural Disasters — The World Is Ending and You're on the Toilet

Nobody rehearses this scenario. Emergency preparedness guides assume you're in
the living room, awake, dressed, and near your go-bag. They do not account for
the possibility that the earthquake, tornado, or fire alarm arrives while you
are mid-flush, barefoot, and holding your phone at 11%. And yet here we are.

**The good news — and there is some — is that the bathroom is not a terrible
place to ride out many emergencies.** In some cases it's actively one of the
better rooms in the building. This chapter explains when that's true, when it
isn't, and what to do either way.

## 1. The Bathroom as Shelter

### Why bathrooms keep showing up in emergency guidance

Emergency agencies frequently recommend small, interior rooms without windows
as shelter during severe weather and some other events.[^fema-safe-room] The
average bathroom checks several of those boxes without trying:

| Feature | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Small footprint | Less ceiling span to collapse. Shorter walls brace each other. |
| Interior location | Often no exterior walls, reducing wind and debris exposure. |
| Reinforced walls | Plumbing stacks require structural backing. The wall behind your toilet is sturdier than the one behind your motivational poster. |
| Hard surfaces | Porcelain and tile don't burn easily and don't generate soft debris. |
| Water access | Taps and toilet tank provide an emergency water source. |
| Door with lock | Privacy during normal times; access control during emergencies. |
| Drain | If water enters, it has somewhere to go (up to a point). |

### When the bathroom is worse than other rooms

Not every bathroom is a bunker. Assess honestly:

- **Large windows or skylights.** Glass is the enemy during high winds,
  earthquakes, and explosions. A bathroom with a floor-to-ceiling window is a
  display case, not a shelter.
- **Upper floors in flood zones.** Good for flooding (higher ground), bad for
  earthquakes and fire (harder to evacuate).
- **No second exit.** Most bathrooms have one door. If that path is blocked,
  you are trapped. Know this going in.
- **Mirrors.** Large mounted mirrors become projectiles during shaking. More on
  this in the earthquake section.
- **Chemical storage.** If the under-sink cabinet is a chemistry set of
  bleach, ammonia, aerosol cans, and nail polish remover, those become
  hazards during fire or structural damage.

### Quick structural assessment (10 seconds)

Before deciding to shelter in the bathroom, scan:

1. **Walls:** Solid or partition? Knock. Hollow drywall vibrates. Concrete,
   tile-over-block, or plumbing walls thud.
2. **Ceiling:** Flat or suspended? Drop ceilings collapse easily but won't
   kill you. Concrete ceilings hold but may crack.
3. **Windows:** Present? Size? Can they be covered or avoided?
4. **Door:** Does it open inward or outward? Debris against an inward-opening
   door traps you. Outward-opening doors can be blocked by fallen material
   on the other side.
5. **Fixtures:** What could fall? Shelving, cabinets, mirrors, light fixtures.

This is not a full engineering survey. It's triage. You have seconds, not a
clipboard.

## 2. Earthquake

### The basics still apply

**Drop. Cover. Hold on.**[^fema-earthquake]

This is not negotiable advice. It is the consensus recommendation from FEMA,
USGS, and every major seismological agency on Earth. The instinct to run is
strong and almost always wrong — most earthquake injuries happen when people
try to move during shaking, not from building collapse.[^usgs-myths]

In the bathroom, "Drop, Cover, Hold On" looks like this:

### The bathtub protocol

If there is a bathtub:

1. **Get in it.** The tub is a rigid porcelain or steel shell anchored to the
   floor and backed by reinforced walls. It is, structurally, one of the
   strongest containers in a residential building.
2. **Lie down or crouch.** Get below the rim. The tub walls provide lateral
   protection from falling debris.
3. **Cover your head and neck.** Use your arms, a towel, a bath mat — anything
   between your skull and whatever is falling.
4. **Hold on to something fixed.** The tub edge, the faucet, the drain. You
   need an anchor point because shaking will try to move you.

If there is no bathtub, get next to the toilet (anchored to the floor and
backed by a reinforced plumbing wall), crouch, and cover your head.

### What to avoid

- **Mirrors.** Move away from large mirrors. They shatter into heavy, sharp
  sheets. A medicine cabinet mirror is less dangerous than a wall-mounted
  vanity mirror, but neither is your friend during a 6.0.
- **Glass shower doors.** If you're in a glass-enclosed shower when shaking
  starts, get out of it if you can do so safely. If you can't, crouch low
  and cover your head.
- **Shelves above the toilet.** Whatever decorative objects seemed like a good
  idea are now projectiles. The ceramic owl is no longer whimsical.

### During shaking

- Stay where you are until shaking stops completely.
- Do not try to run out of the building.
- Do not stand in a doorway. (This advice is from the 1930s and applies to
  unreinforced adobe structures. Your bathroom doorframe is not magic.)
- Expect it to last 10–60 seconds. It will feel like an hour.
- If something falls on you and you're not pinned, shield your head and wait.

### After shaking stops

1. **Assess yourself.** Check for injuries. Adrenaline masks pain — look for
   blood, deformity, inability to move a limb.
2. **Check for hazards.** Smell gas? Hear hissing? See water spraying? Cracked
   walls? Sagging ceiling?
3. **Shoes.** If your shoes are in the bathroom, put them on. If not, wrap
   your feet in towels before walking through debris. Broken glass on tile
   is invisible until it isn't.
4. **Open the door carefully.** If it's jammed, don't force it — you may
   destabilise the frame. Try lifting the door slightly while pulling.
5. **Expect aftershocks.** They will come. The first significant aftershock
   typically arrives within minutes to hours of the main event. When one
   hits, repeat the protocol: drop, cover, hold on.
6. **Do not flush the toilet.** Seriously. The sewer line may be broken. You
   don't want to find out the hard way.

### Water supply

The bathroom gives you immediate water access, which matters enormously after
an earthquake when mains may be compromised. Act before the supply runs out:

- **Fill the bathtub** immediately after shaking stops. Even if you don't
  think you'll need it. Water pressure may drop within minutes as mains
  break elsewhere.
- **Fill every available container.** Cups, bottles, the sink with the drain
  plugged.
- **The toilet tank** (the reservoir behind the seat, not the bowl) contains
  several litres of clean water. See Section 6 for details.

## 3. Severe Weather

### Tornado

The bathroom may be the single best room in the building during a tornado.
This is not a guess — FEMA specifically recommends small interior rooms on
the lowest floor, and bathrooms are named as an example.[^fema-tornado]

**Why the bathroom works:**

- Interior location shields from wind-borne debris.
- Small room means short wall spans and mutual bracing.
- Plumbing walls are reinforced.
- The bathtub provides a rigid enclosure below standing height.
- Water pipes running through walls add structural steel to the wall
  assembly.

**What to do:**

1. Get to the lowest-floor bathroom if possible.
2. Get into the bathtub. Pull a mattress over you if one is accessible and
   you have time. Otherwise: pillows, couch cushions, heavy blankets,
   sleeping bags — anything to absorb impact from debris.
3. Cover your head and neck.
4. Stay away from mirrors and windows.
5. Leave the door open slightly — pressure equalisation matters. A sealed
   room during a tornado can experience rapid pressure changes that stress
   the structure.
6. Wait for the all-clear. Tornado passage typically lasts seconds to a few
   minutes. The silence after can be deceptive — confirm via weather radio,
   phone alerts, or official channels before emerging.

**The EF scale and what it means for your bathroom:**

| EF Rating | Wind Speed | Your bathroom |
|---|---|---|
| EF0–EF1 | 105–180 km/h | Intact. You'll hear it. Debris on roof. |
| EF2 | 181–250 km/h | Roof damage possible. Interior rooms hold. |
| EF3 | 251–330 km/h | Upper floors compromised. Ground floor interior rooms are your best option. |
| EF4–EF5 | 331+ km/h | Catastrophic. Only below-grade shelter reliably survives. The bathroom is better than nothing but not a guarantee. |

### Hurricane

Hurricanes give you more warning than tornadoes. Use that time.

**Before the storm:**

- **Fill the bathtub with water.** Not for bathing — for survival. A standard
  bathtub holds 150–300 litres. That's drinking water, flushing water,
  cleaning water for days.[^red-cross-hurricane]
- Fill every other container you can find.
- Charge your phone. Then charge it again.
- Move away from windows. If the bathroom has a window, cover it or avoid
  that bathroom.

**During the storm:**

- Stay in the interior bathroom.
- If the building has multiple stories, stay on the lower floor (wind) but
  above ground floor if storm surge or flooding is expected (water). Yes,
  this is a contradiction. Resolve it based on your specific risk — coastal
  areas favour going up, inland areas favour staying low.
- Do not open windows "to equalise pressure." This is a myth. Opening
  windows lets in wind and water and makes structural failure more
  likely.[^noaa-myths]
- The eye of the storm is not the end. If everything goes calm, stay put.
  The back half is coming.

### Flooding

Flooding inverts the bathroom calculus:

- **Ground-floor bathroom:** This is no longer shelter. This is a room with
  one exit that is filling with water. Get out and go up.
- **Upper-floor bathroom:** Viable shelter if the building structure is sound.
  You have water access, a lockable door, and elevation.
- **Basement bathroom:** Leave immediately. Basements flood first.

**If water is rising around you:**

1. Move to the highest accessible point.
2. Do not walk through moving water. Fifteen centimetres of fast-moving water
   can knock an adult down. Sixty centimetres can float a car.[^nws-flood]
3. Do not touch electrical outlets, switches, or appliances if water is
   present.
4. If trapped by rising water, signal from the highest point. Break a window
   if necessary to signal rescuers.
5. Never try to swim through floodwater to reach an exit unless the
   alternative is drowning. Floodwater contains debris, chemicals, sewage,
   and currents that don't respect your swimming ability.

## 4. Fire

Fire is the emergency where the bathroom is most likely to become a trap
rather than a shelter. Everything depends on where the fire is relative to
you and whether your escape route is clear.

### First: Can you leave?

**Feel the door.** Use the back of your hand — palm-side burns damage your
grip, which you may need. Feel:

- The door itself (centre and edges)
- The handle
- The frame

| What you feel | What it means | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Cool | Fire is not immediately outside | Open carefully, stay low, check the hallway. If clear: evacuate. |
| Warm | Fire is nearby but not against the door | Open very carefully, stay below smoke. Evaluate. Be ready to close it. |
| Hot | Fire is on the other side | **Do not open.** The bathroom is now your refuge room. |

### The bathroom as refuge room

If you cannot leave:

1. **Close the door completely.** A closed door can hold back fire and smoke
   for 20–60 minutes depending on construction.[^ul-door]
2. **Seal the gap.** Wet towels along the bottom of the door. Wet them
   thoroughly — a damp towel is a filter; a dry towel is fuel.
3. **Seal any vents.** Stuff wet cloth into ventilation grilles if smoke is
   entering through them.
4. **Turn on the shower** — cold water — and let it run. The steam and
   moisture saturate the air, reducing smoke density and cooling the
   room.[^nfpa-shelter]
5. **Stay low.** Smoke rises. The cleanest air is near the floor.
6. **Signal your location.** If there's a window: open it from the top if
   possible (smoke out), signal from the bottom. Hang a towel or piece of
   clothing outside. If no window: bang on pipes or walls at regular
   intervals.
7. **Call emergency services** and tell them exactly where you are:
   building, floor, which side, the bathroom. "I'm in the second-floor
   bathroom, north side of the building" saves minutes.

### Bathroom-specific fire risks

Your bathroom may contain more flammable and explosive material than you
think:

| Item | Risk |
|---|---|
| Aerosol cans (hairspray, deodorant, air freshener) | Pressurised and flammable. Can explode in heat. Move away from the door and heat source. |
| Nail polish remover (acetone) | Highly flammable, volatile. |
| Rubbing alcohol / isopropanol | Flash point 12 °C. It's basically waiting for an excuse. |
| Cleaning products (bleach + ammonia) | Not flammable, but heat can release toxic chloramine gas. Do not mix. Ever.[^cdc-cleaning] |
| Hair dryer / straightener | Electrical ignition source if left plugged in near water or fabric. |
| Toilet paper / paper towels | Obvious fuel source. |
| Bath oils | Flammable. The romantic evening has taken a turn. |

**If any of these are near the heat source**, move them to the far side of
the room if you can do so safely. If you can't, leave them and stay distant.

### When the bathroom is a trap

The bathroom becomes a trap when:

- Fire is between you and the only exit **and** the room has no window.
- Smoke is entering faster than you can seal it.
- The room is heating rapidly (the door, walls, or floor are hot).
- You're on a high floor with no way for rescuers to reach the window.

In these cases, evacuation — even through smoke — may be necessary. Stay
below the smoke line, cover your nose and mouth with a wet cloth, and move
toward a known exit. Do not stop to collect belongings. Do not use the
lift.

## 5. Building Emergency

### Power outage

You're in a windowless interior room and the lights just went out. This is
disorienting but not dangerous in itself.

**Immediate actions:**

1. **Don't move yet.** A bathroom has hard edges, wet surfaces, and sharp
   corners. Moving in darkness invites injury.
2. **Phone flashlight.** Your phone is almost certainly within arm's reach.
   Use it.
3. **Stay put briefly.** Power outages in buildings are often momentary or
   affect emergency lighting circuits. Give it 30 seconds.
4. **If power doesn't return**, finish what you're doing and leave the
   bathroom. Check the hallway — if emergency lighting is on, the building
   systems are working. If the entire building is dark, proceed cautiously
   toward exits.
5. **Do not use candles** in a small enclosed room with paper products and
   aerosol cans. See the fire section above for why.

**Public/commercial buildings:** Exit signs should be illuminated by battery
backup. Follow them. Lifts are not an option — use stairs.

### Gas leak

If you smell gas (natural gas is odourised with mercaptan — a sulphurous,
rotten-egg smell):

1. **Do not flip any switches.** No lights on, no lights off, no fan, no
   exhaust. Electrical sparks ignite gas.[^gas-safety]
2. **Do not flush the toilet.** Some flush mechanisms create sparks.
3. **Open a window** if the bathroom has one.
4. **Leave the building.** Do not stop to investigate. Do not call from
   inside — use your phone outside.
5. **Call emergency services and your gas utility** from outside.

If you're in a public building and smell gas, alert staff on your way out.
Don't pull fire alarms for gas leaks — fire suppression systems (sprinklers)
are unhelpful and the alarm may cause spark-generating actions.

### Structural damage

After an explosion, earthquake, vehicle impact, or partial collapse:

**Assess before moving:**

| Check | Look for | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Ceiling | Cracks, sagging, fallen material | Possible ongoing collapse |
| Walls | New cracks, leaning, gaps at joints | Structural compromise |
| Floor | Tilting, holes, buckling | Load-bearing failure below |
| Door | Jammed, frame distorted | Building has shifted |
| Water | Spraying, pooling, discoloured | Broken pipes |
| Dust | Thick airborne particulate | Ongoing structural movement |
| Sounds | Creaking, groaning, cracking | The building is still moving — stay low |

**Decision rule:** If you see or hear active structural movement (ongoing
cracking, dust falling, floor tilting), stay in the bathroom and protect
your head. Wait for the movement to stop. If the building is stable but
damaged, evacuate via stairs, staying near load-bearing walls (typically
the ones with plumbing).

## 6. Water and Utilities

The bathroom is the only room in most buildings where you have direct,
immediate access to multiple water sources. In a sustained emergency, this
matters more than anything else in this chapter.

### Available water sources

| Source | Approximate volume | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bathtub (filled) | 150–300 L | Your primary reservoir. Fill it early. |
| Toilet tank (cistern) | 6–12 L | Clean water — same supply as the tap. Not the bowl. Never the bowl. |
| Sink tap | Continuous while pressure holds | Fill containers while you can. |
| Hot water heater (if accessible) | 100–200 L | The largest single water source in most buildings. Usually not in the bathroom, but worth knowing. |
| Shower head | Continuous while pressure holds | Same supply as tap; harder to fill containers from. |

### The toilet tank: your least appetising but most reliable backup

The toilet tank — the porcelain reservoir mounted behind the seat — contains
water from the same supply line as your tap. It is not sewage. It has not
touched the bowl. It is, for practical purposes, tap water that has been
sitting in a porcelain container.[^cdc-water]

**To access it:**

1. Lift the tank lid. Set it down carefully — it's heavy ceramic and will
   shatter on tile.
2. Do not use water from tanks that contain coloured cleaning tablets or
   chemical blocks (blue, green, or otherwise tinted water). Those chemicals
   are not for drinking.
3. If the tank water is clear and untreated, it's drinkable in an emergency.
   For extended use, purify it.

### Water purification (emergency methods)

If you're unsure about water safety — and after an earthquake or flood, you
should be unsure — basic purification options:[^epa-water]

| Method | How | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Boiling | Rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 2,000 m altitude) | Kills bacteria, viruses, parasites. The gold standard. |
| Household bleach (unscented, 6–8.25% sodium hypochlorite) | 8 drops per gallon (≈4 L), stir, wait 30 minutes. Water should smell slightly of chlorine. | Kills most bacteria and viruses. Less effective against parasites. |
| UV exposure | Clear bottle in direct sunlight for 6+ hours | SODIS method. Works but slow. Requires a clear container and sunlight. Not useful if you're in an interior room. |
| Commercial filter/tablets | Per manufacturer instructions | If you happen to have them. You probably don't. |

**You cannot purify water by:**
- Filtering it through a t-shirt (removes sediment, not pathogens)
- Adding alcohol (you'd need concentrations that would harm you first)
- Freezing it (parasites laugh at your freezer)
- Wishing

### When plumbing is compromised

After earthquakes, floods, or structural damage, the plumbing system may be
broken even if water still flows:

- **Discoloured water** (brown, yellow, milky) — do not drink without
  purification. Sediment may indicate broken mains.
- **Loss of pressure** — fill containers immediately. Pressure loss means
  the system is draining somewhere.
- **Sewage smell from drains** — the sewer line may be cracked. Stop using
  toilets and sinks until assessed. Back-flow can contaminate your water
  supply.
- **Hot water but no cold** (or vice versa) — one supply line is broken. Use
  whichever works while it works.

### Storage

If you anticipate a sustained emergency:

1. Plug the bathtub drain and fill it. Cover with a clean sheet to reduce
   contamination.
2. Fill every container: cups, bottles, pots, plastic bags (doubled).
3. Ration: a minimum of 2 litres per person per day for drinking, more if
   active or hot.[^who-water]
4. Label containers with the date and source if you have a marker.
5. Use the cleanest-sourced water first (tap > tank > questionable).

## 7. Communication and Rescue

### Phone signal in interior rooms

Bathrooms are often the worst room in a building for phone signal. Interior
walls, plumbing infrastructure, and tile reduce signal strength. This is
not a design flaw — it's physics being unhelpful.

**Improve your odds:**

- Hold the phone near the ceiling (signal penetrates roofs more easily than
  walls).
- Try near the door rather than deep in the room.
- Text rather than call — texts require less sustained signal and will queue
  for delivery.[^fcc-emergency]
- Try Wi-Fi calling if connected to a network.
- If you have no signal at all, try calling emergency numbers (112, 911,
  999). These connect through any available network, not just your
  carrier.[^itu-emergency]

### Making noise for rescuers

If you're trapped and your phone is dead or has no signal:

- **Bang on pipes.** Metal pipes transmit sound through walls and floors
  far more effectively than shouting. Use a hard object (shampoo bottle,
  toilet lid, shoe) and hit the pipes in a regular pattern: three knocks,
  pause, three knocks.
- **Blow a whistle** if you have one. (You don't. But if you ever restock
  your bathroom emergency kit, add one.)
- **Shout periodically, not continuously.** Continuous shouting exhausts you
  in minutes and isn't more audible than regular intervals. Shout when you
  hear activity outside.
- **Tap on the floor** if you're above ground level. Rescuers often work
  from below.

### Leaving signs of occupation

If you need to leave the bathroom temporarily (to find a better exit, reach
supplies, or assess the building):

- Leave the light on (if power is available).
- Write a note: "PERSON HERE — [time] — [your name]." Leave it visible.
- Hang a towel from the door handle.
- Leave the door ajar but blocked (so it doesn't close and lock behind you).

### Emergency numbers by region

| Region | Primary | Fire | Police | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EU / most of Europe | 112 | 112 | 112 | Single number, all services. Works on any phone, even without a SIM. |
| Germany | 112 | 112 | 110 | 112 for fire/medical, 110 for police. |
| United Kingdom | 999 | 999 | 999 | 112 also works. |
| United States | 911 | 911 | 911 | Text-to-911 available in many areas. |
| Canada | 911 | 911 | 911 | 112 redirects to 911 on some carriers. |
| Australia | 000 | 000 | 000 | 112 from mobiles also works. |
| Japan | 119 (fire/medical) | 119 | 110 | Different numbers for different services. |
| International (mobile) | 112 | — | — | GSM standard. Works in most countries from any mobile phone. |

When calling, state clearly:

1. What happened (earthquake, fire, trapped, injured).
2. Where you are (address, floor, room, side of building).
3. How many people are with you.
4. What injuries exist.
5. Do not hang up until told to.

## 8. After the Event

### Before you move

The immediate aftermath is when people get hurt by acting too quickly. The
event is over; your adrenaline says run; the correct answer is almost always
"assess first."

**Injury check:**

- Check yourself head to toe. Adrenaline suppresses pain — you may be cut,
  bruised, or have a fracture you haven't noticed.
- If someone else is with you, check them.
- Apply basic first aid (pressure on bleeding, immobilise suspected
  fractures). See the Self Ambulance Guide if available.
- Do not remove large debris from wounds — it may be controlling the
  bleeding.

**Environment check:**

| Hazard | Detection | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Gas leak | Smell (rotten egg / sulphur) | Leave immediately. Do not use switches. |
| Electrical | Sparking, burning smell, exposed wires | Do not touch. Do not step in water near electrical sources. |
| Structural | Visible cracks, tilting, ongoing sounds | Stay in shelter. Wait for movement to stop. |
| Water/sewage | Flooding, sewage smell, discoloured water | Move up. Do not walk through contaminated water. |
| Fire/smoke | Visible smoke, burning smell, heat | Follow fire protocol from Section 4. |
| Chemical | Strong chemical odour, spilled containers | Ventilate. Move away. Do not mix or clean up. |

### Evacuation vs. shelter in place

**Evacuate when:**

- The building is on fire or filling with smoke.
- Gas is leaking and you can reach an exit.
- The structure is actively collapsing.
- Authorities have ordered evacuation.
- Floodwater is rising and you cannot go higher.

**Shelter in place when:**

- Aftershocks are continuing (earthquake).
- The air outside is more hazardous than inside (chemical release, fallout).
- The building is damaged but stable and you are not in immediate danger.
- You are injured and moving would worsen the injury.
- You cannot safely reach an exit.

When in doubt: if the building is not on fire and not actively collapsing,
staying in a stable interior room is usually safer than navigating a damaged
building in the dark.

### Documentation

This feels absurd when you're covered in dust and your hands are shaking.
Do it anyway:

1. **Photograph damage** — your injuries, the room, the building. Insurance
   and emergency services both need this.
2. **Note the time.** Timestamps matter for medical treatment and official
   reports.
3. **Write down what happened** while it's fresh. Memory degrades quickly
   after traumatic events, and you may need to give statements later.
4. **Save your phone battery** for calls, not social media updates. The
   dramatic selfie can wait. Your battery cannot.

### Reunification

If you're separated from others:

- Designate a meeting point outside the building (this is ideally planned
  before an emergency, but "the car" or "across the street" works in
  retrospect).
- Register with local disaster reunification services if available (Red Cross
  Safe and Well, Google Person Finder during activations).
- Do not re-enter a damaged building to find someone. Report their last
  known location to emergency services.

### The next 24 hours

- Expect aftershocks (earthquake), secondary weather events (hurricane),
  or delayed structural failures.
- Drink water. Eat something. Both are easy to forget when your body is in
  survival mode.
- Contact your insurance provider, landlord, or building management when
  safe to do so.
- Monitor official channels — local emergency management, weather service,
  utility providers.
- Allow yourself to feel terrible. You just survived a disaster in a
  bathroom. That's a legitimate story. Nobody's trauma requires a minimum
  square footage.
