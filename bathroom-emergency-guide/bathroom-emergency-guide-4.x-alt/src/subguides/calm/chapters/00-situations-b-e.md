# Situations B–G — Pick the Closest Door

Anxiety, pain, danger, overload, smells, and nowhere-to-go states can overlap,
but they are not the same problem. They share a family resemblance in the same
way that rain, tears, and a leaking pipe all involve water — acknowledging the
overlap does not mean one umbrella fixes everything.

Pick the closest door. Learn something useful about it. Borrow tools from
another branch when reality refuses to respect chapter boundaries, which it
will.

## B — I feel anxious

### Split the experience before trying to solve it

Anxiety arrives as a bundle. Your nervous system does not ship separate
notifications for each component — it fires everything at once and lets you
sort it out. So let's sort it out.

Separate four layers:

| Layer | Example | Useful question |
|---|---|---|
| sensation | racing heart, nausea, tingling | where is it? wave or steady? |
| emotion | fear, shame, dread | one word is enough |
| prediction | "this will become unbearable" | is that an event, or a forecast? |
| impulse | flee, check, text, hide | what happens if it waits one minute? |

Now change one variable. Sit. Loosen clothing pressure. Turn down one sound.
Look away from the mirror. Contact one person. Compare after a minute. The
point is not to win an argument with anxiety — anxiety has infinite rebuttals
and no closing time. The point is to stop four different phenomena from
impersonating one enormous fact.

**New, severe, unusual, or rapidly worsening physical symptoms belong to medical
assessment rather than an anxiety experiment.** When in doubt, treat the body
first. You can always re-file it as anxiety later; you cannot un-ignore a
cardiac event.

### Panic attack — when anxiety goes supernova

Panic can include racing heart, chest discomfort, dizziness, tingling,
trembling, sweating, nausea, derealization, fear of losing control, or fear
of dying. The sensations are real even when the cause is panic. Your body is
not making this up — it's making an error of scale, which is a different
problem entirely.

The difficulty is that some emergency conditions can feel similar. Therefore:

- new, severe, unusual, or rapidly worsening symptoms get medical assessment;
- known recurrent panic with no red flag may use the Calm Guide (Ch.4);
- uncertainty is allowed to escalate to **112** or **116 117**.

Do not make "prove this is only panic" your first task. Your nervous system has
already opened enough tabs.

### GAD-7 — a useful ruler with known flex

The GAD-7 asks how often seven anxiety symptoms occurred during the previous
**two weeks**. Scores are commonly grouped as:

| Score | Label | What it suggests |
|---:|---|---|
| 0–4 | minimal | recent symptom burden is low |
| 5–9 | mild | worth monitoring; discuss if it's getting in the way |
| 10–14 | moderate | professional assessment is a reasonable idea |
| 15–21 | severe | prompt professional assessment is a reasonable idea |

The score is a screening and communication tool. It is **not** an acute panic
triage score, cannot exclude a physical emergency, and does not turn a person
into one of four coloured rectangles.[^gad7]

About that ruler's flex: the original 2006 primary-care study reported 89%
sensitivity and 82% specificity at a cut-off of 10. A later Cochrane review
found a pooled 64% sensitivity and 91% specificity for generalized anxiety
disorder at the same cut-off.[^gad7-review] That is not a scandal. It is what
happens when one instrument leaves its development sample and meets different
settings, populations, and reference standards. A high score supports
assessment; a low score does not prove absence; neither result handles acute
medical triage.

Think of it as a thermometer that's good at confirming fever but occasionally
says "fine" when you're not. Useful — just not infallible.

### What the stress response is doing

Threat detection can increase heart rate, breathing, muscle tension, vigilance,
and the urge to flee, fight, freeze, appease, or hide. Attention narrows
toward possible danger. This is useful when a tiger has entered the bathroom
and less useful when the tiger is an email.

A practical way to walk through it:

| Layer | Question | Example action |
|---|---|---|
| Body | What is the alarm system doing? | sit, orient, breathe at a pace that feels okay |
| Environment | Is danger actually present? | check door, smoke, person, symptoms |
| Thought | What prediction is looping? | "I notice the thought that..." |
| Action | What helps in five minutes? | water, call, leave, medical help |
| Pattern | Is this recurring or shrinking life? | arrange professional support |

The bottom row matters. A stress response that fires once during a weird party
is a story. A stress response that fires every Tuesday and has started
cancelling your plans is a pattern, and patterns deserve professional
attention — not because you're broken, but because patterns respond better to
structured help than to willpower and deep breaths.

## C — I feel pain

### Do now

Call **112** for sudden severe pain, chest pressure, severe abdominal pain,
major injury, collapse, severe breathlessness, new neurological symptoms,
pregnancy-related red flags, or serious uncertainty.

Without a red flag:

- stop the activity that worsens it;
- sit or lie safely;
- note where and when it began;
- use simple first aid from Ch.5;
- contact a practice or **116 117** when it's urgent but not life-threatening.

### NRS pain scale — communication, not diagnosis

A 0–10 rating helps communicate experience. It does not directly measure tissue
damage, blood chemistry, or urgency. A person can have a dangerous condition
with modest pain and severe pain without life-threatening injury. The number is
a shared vocabulary, not a verdict.

| Rating | Plain-language description |
|---:|---|
| 0 | no pain |
| 1–3 | present but relatively manageable |
| 4–6 | meaningfully interferes with activity or concentration |
| 7–9 | severe, dominates attention or function |
| 10 | worst pain the person can imagine or report |

Change over time is useful too. "It was a 3 this morning and it's a 7 now"
tells a responder more than either number alone. Track direction, not decimal
places.

### Better pain description — OPQRST

If you can manage it, record:

- **O — Onset:** sudden or gradual? exact time?
- **P — Provokes / palliates:** movement, breathing, food, position?
- **Q — Quality:** pressure, burning, stabbing, cramping, aching?
- **R — Region / radiation:** where? does it spread?
- **S — Severity:** number plus effect on function?
- **T — Time:** constant, waves, worsening, recurring?

Add injury, pregnancy, fever, vomiting, weakness, numbness, rash, medication,
and relevant medical history. This makes a better phone call than "my left side
is being weird, please infer the plot."

## D — I feel endangered

### Danger is present now

- Move toward an exit, other people, or a lockable safe place — whichever
  reduces danger rather than trapping you.
- Keep noise and screen light low if discovery creates risk.
- Call **110** for police. Call **112** for medical rescue or immediate danger
  to life.
- Do not confront the person to obtain a cleaner narrative.
- Take children, medication, phone, keys, and mobility aids only when doing so
  does not increase danger.

If you are reading this section preventively: good. If you are reading it
right now in real danger: skip the rest and act on the bullet points above.
Come back for the planning section when you are safe.

### The danger is a memory, message, or expected encounter

Not all danger is a person in the room. Sometimes it is a text message,
a pattern of escalation, or a date on a calendar.

- Tell one trusted person.
- Save messages, images, dates, and witness names if safe to do so.
- Plan transport and a place to stay.
- Change passwords and device access from a trusted device if monitoring is
  possible. Use a device they do not control and a network they cannot see.
- Contact a specialist service. The Violence against Women Helpline is
  **116 016** — anonymous, free, multilingual, available around the clock.[^violence-help]

A safety plan is not a promise that you will execute it perfectly. It is a map
available when thinking becomes expensive. Fill it in during a calm moment.
Store it where the person who frightens you cannot find it.

### The body after danger

Shaking, nausea, numbness, crying, anger, insomnia, startle, and looping
memory can continue after the threat ends. These responses do not prove ongoing
danger or prove safety — they show that the nervous system has not received the
final memo. This is normal. It is deeply unpleasant and also normal.

Use the Calm Guide (Ch.4) after checking actual safety. Seek professional help
when symptoms are severe, persistent, functionally impairing, or connected to
self-harm.

## E — Things are congesting

Overload often feels like every task became urgent at once. They did not. Your
brain is a single-threaded processor pretending to be a server farm, and it
has hit capacity.

### Cognitive load — why the brain is crashing

A practical model:

$$L = I + E + S$$

where:

- $I$ = intrinsic difficulty of the task itself;
- $E$ = avoidable clutter — interruptions, ambiguity, seventeen open tabs;
- $S$ = stress, pain, fatigue, hunger, or emotional load.

You may not be able to reduce $I$ right now. You can often reduce $E$ or $S$:
close tabs, silence notifications, write the task down instead of holding it
in RAM, drink water, eat something, sit, ask another person to hold one
responsibility, or stop trying to solve Thursday while it is still
Tuesday.[^cowan]

The equation is not physics. It is a reminder that cognitive capacity is not
fixed and mysterious — it has inputs you can actually touch.

### Sleep debt can hide behind confidence

A controlled study followed 48 healthy adults across 14 days of restricted
sleep. The four- and six-hour groups showed cumulative, dose-dependent
performance deficits. The interesting part: subjective sleepiness rose early
but then plateaued, while objective impairment kept climbing. People did not
become accurate judges of their own tiredness merely by becoming familiar with
feeling tired.[^sleep-loss]

One small lab study is not a personal impairment calculator. But it does
support a polite operational rule: after repeated short sleep, reduce complex
choices, write down handoffs, double-check medication and driving decisions,
and borrow a second alert brain where possible. Coffee may attend the meeting;
it does not get voting rights on whether you are fully restored.

### The three-line triage

When everything is urgent, write three lines:

1. **Must prevent harm today**
2. **Must happen soon**
3. **Can survive being ugly**

Now choose one action under five minutes. Just one. The congestion did not
arrive as a single mass and it does not leave as one either.

### The congestion board

| Task | Harm if delayed | Deadline | Owner | Next physical action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | low / medium / high | | | |
| | low / medium / high | | | |
| | low / medium / high | | | |

A "next physical action" is visible and executable: "open the letter," "call
the practice," "put the keys in the bag." Note that "fix life" is not yet an
action. It is an aspiration with no physical form, and it will stay on the list
forever, generating guilt. Break it down or cross it out.

### Five-minute reboot

1. Put every task on paper. All of them. The paper can hold more than you can.
2. Circle anything involving safety, shelter, medication, children, animals, or
   a deadline today.
3. Pick one circle.
4. Do only the first visible action within that circle.
5. Reassess after five minutes.

If basic care is repeatedly failing — meals skipped, hygiene abandoned, bills
unopened for weeks — involve professional, social, or practical support.
Productivity advice is not a substitute for treating depression, ADHD, burnout,
sleep deprivation, pain, or crisis. Sometimes the system needs a technician,
not a new to-do app.

## F — Bad smell, after the hazard gate

### The rule

A smell is not permission to investigate from inside the plume. Before you play
detective, ask: **fire, smoke, CO alarm, gas, electrical burning, unknown
fumes, chemical exposure, or symptoms?** If yes, maybe, or unclear, use
**Situation H** and move away before troubleshooting.

Do **not** light a match to diagnose or "neutralize" an unknown smell. Do not
operate switches when gas may be present. Do not mix cleaning products. None
of these are controversial instructions, yet all of them get violated on a
surprisingly regular basis.

### Smell decision tree

| Smell or sign | Safe first action | Destination |
|---|---|---|
| gas / rotten egg, hissing | no flame, switch, bell, fan, or phone inside; knock on neighbours' doors, leave | Situation H → gas service / **112** |
| smoke / burning plastic / electrical | leave; cut power only from a safely reachable panel | Situation H → **112** |
| sharp chemical / mixed cleaner / eye or airway irritation | stop exposure; fresh air; rinse exposed skin and eyes | Situation H → poison centre / **112** |
| solvent / fuel | leave the source area; prevent ignition | Situation H → specialist / **112** if danger |
| sewage / drain, no acute symptoms | run water into an unused trap; ventilate | plumber or building management if persistent |
| damp / musty | inspect moisture without disturbing heavy mould | landlord or building specialist; GP for symptoms |
| ordinary toilet smell | flush, close lid, ventilate, clean | no combustion-based innovation required |
| unexplained body or breath odour with illness | assess the person, not merely the smell | GP / 116 117; **112** for severe signs |

### Cleaning chemistry: the minimal doctrine

- Use one product at a time.
- Follow the label's dilution and ventilation instructions. The label was
  written by someone whose job is to stop you from gassing yourself. Respect
  their work.
- Never combine chlorine bleach with acids, toilet cleaner, vinegar, or
  ammonia-containing products.
- Store products in their original labelled containers.
- If exposure occurred: move to fresh air if safe, rinse skin and eyes as
  directed, keep the product label, and contact **112** or a poison centre
  based on symptoms.

### Persistent drain smell

A dry trap in a rarely used sink, shower, or floor drain can allow sewer gas
into the room. Running water for a few seconds may refill the trap and solve
the problem. Persistent odour, leaks, or repeatedly empty traps need building
management or plumbing repair.

A candle is not plumbing, even when scented "Alpine Responsibility."

## G — No place to go

Use the dedicated **Situation G — No Safe Place** chapter. It separates active
violence, no roof tonight, inaccessible or medically unusable accommodation,
and social or internal crisis into distinct branches, because "no place to go"
can mean anything from "the landlord changed the locks" to "I have a roof but
nowhere I feel safe."

The first action is still to secure the next safe hour. The dedicated chapter
names the matching German and local service routes.

## Situations B–G — consolidated routing map

When in doubt, use this table. Find your row, do the "do now" column, and
read the rest when you can.

| Situation | Do now | Understand later | Escalate when |
|---|---|---|---|
| B — Anxiety | orient outward; contact one person | layer separation, GAD-7, stress response | red flag, self-harm risk, pattern worsening |
| C — Pain | check red flags; simple first aid | OPQRST description, pain log | sudden / severe / neuro / chest / collapse |
| D — Danger | move to safety; call 110 / 112 | safety planning, body-after-danger | threat is current or injury exists |
| E — Congestion | externalize tasks; choose one action | cognitive load, sleep debt, delegation | basic care or safety is failing |
| F — Smell | run Situation H hazard gate first; leave fumes | source-specific troubleshooting after clearance | fire, gas, chemical exposure, symptoms |
| G — No place | use Situation G chapter; secure one safe hour | violence / no-roof / access / social branches | exposure, violence, failed essential care, medical or self-harm risk |
