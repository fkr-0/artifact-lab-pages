# Calm Guide — You Made It Here. That Counts.

This guide is referenced by almost every path in this document. That's not
because it solves everything — it's because calm is a prerequisite for solving
anything. You can't navigate a decision tree while your nervous system is
running kernel panic. Let's get you to a stable state first, then figure out
what's next.

## You're Allowed to Be Here

**Permission statement.** Lock the door. Sit down. **You are in a room designed
specifically for private bodily maintenance. Your brain is a body part. You are
maintaining it.** This is not avoidance — this is triage.

**The 10-minute rule.** The next 10 minutes are officially **Me Time**. Not
because your problems aren't real, but because your nervous system needs a reset
window before it can process anything useful. Think of it as rebooting in safe
mode. Nothing dramatic happens in safe mode. That's the point.

**Where you are, factually:**

- Indoors ✓ (weather is not your problem right now)
- Seated or can sit ✓ (gravity: manageable)
- Door locks ✓ (privacy: available)
- Running water ✓ (hydration + hygiene: accessible)
- Reading this ✓ (cognitive function: operational, even if barely)

You have more resources than your anxiety is letting you see. That's what
anxiety does — it narrows the aperture. We're widening it back up.

## The 90-Second Landing

### 1. Make contact

Sit or lean somewhere stable. Feel the floor, wall, or sink supporting your
weight. Unclench your jaw. Drop the shoulders one centimetre — not spiritually,
literally.

### 2. Ground outward (5-4-3-2-1)

Find:

- **5** visible objects
- **4** contact points with your body
- **3** sounds
- **2** colours
- **1** next action

Grounding is an attention task. It doesn't need to produce enlightenment — it
just gives the alarm system something ordinary to process.[^who-stress]

### 3. Breathe comfortably

Try a quiet inhale and a slightly longer, unforced exhale. No giant gulps, no
competition, no heroic breath-holding. If it makes you dizzy or more frightened,
stop and return to normal breathing. You've done enough.

## Breathing Exercises

**Your breath is the one autonomic function you can also control manually.** It's
the backdoor into your own nervous system. Use it.

If any technique makes you dizzy, tingly, or more frightened — **stop and return
to normal breathing. You've done enough.** The best breathing exercise is the one
that doesn't turn breathing into another exam.

![Breathing Techniques — Box Breathing, 4-7-8, Physiological Sigh](build/diagrams/breathing_techniques.png)

### Technique 1: Longer Exhale

The simplest technique. Inhale gently for about 3 counts, exhale for about 4 or
5. Repeat only while comfortable. Skip counting if counting becomes another
supervisor.

### Technique 2: Box Breathing (4-4-4-4)

Used by Navy SEALs, which means it works under conditions significantly worse
than "sitting in a bathroom." The geometry is simple:

```
        4 seconds
    ┌──────────────┐
    │              │
    │   INHALE     │ HOLD
    │              │
4   │              │   4
sec │              │ sec
    │              │
    │  EXHALE      │ HOLD
    │              │
    └──────────────┘
        4 seconds
```

1. Inhale through the nose for 4 counts.
2. Hold for 4 counts.
3. Exhale through the mouth for 4 counts.
4. Hold for 4 counts.
5. Repeat 4 cycles (total: ~64 seconds). Don't rush the counts. If 4 is too
   long, use 3. If 4 is too easy, use 5. The box shape matters more than the
   number.

### Technique 3: 4-7-8 Breathing

A slightly more aggressive calming technique. Good for when box breathing feels
too structured and you just need to slow down.

1. Inhale through the nose for 4 counts.
2. Hold for 7 counts.
3. Exhale through the mouth (audibly) for 8 counts.
4. Repeat 3 cycles. If you feel lightheaded, return to normal breathing —
   you've done enough.

### Technique 4: Physiological Sigh (Emergency Use)

Fastest known way to reduce real-time physiological arousal. Two quick inhales
through the nose, one long exhale through the mouth. Even one round works.
Do 2–3 if you can.[^balban]

```
    Inhale  →  Inhale  →  Exhale
    (short)    (top-up)    (long)
    ···→       ·→          ←←←←←←
```

The double inhale reinflates alveoli in your lungs, increasing CO₂ offload on
the exhale. Your heart rate drops. It's biology, not magic — but it works like
magic.

### Technique 5: No Technique

Normal breathing while noticing external objects is a valid choice. Sometimes
the best thing you can do for your nervous system is stop giving it homework.

## Stress Doesn't Obey a Cinematic Decay Curve

A tidy exponential "cortisol decay" would be fiction. Human arousal isn't a bath
plug: stress chemistry, interpretation, pain, safety, sleep, substances, and new
events interact. The honest model is simpler: several small reductions can matter
even when calm doesn't arrive in one magnificent wave accompanied by tasteful
strings.

## Panic Attacks Have a Shape

If this is a panic attack, it has a characteristic profile: it rises fast, peaks
within minutes, and subsides. It doesn't stay at a flat unchanging maximum
forever, even though it feels like it will.[^panic-course] "This has been at
maximum forever" is worth checking against a clock. A timer is not more
courageous than you — it's simply less anxious.

That does **not** make every chest sensation panic, and it does not make
unfamiliar symptoms safe. If symptoms are new, worsening, or accompanied by a
red flag — use the Self Ambulance Guide.

## Cold Water: Interesting, Not Magical

A cool wet cloth on the face can activate part of the mammalian diving response
and may slow heart rate. It's a useful sensory change.[^cold-face]

But cold exposure is not one single "reset." Full cold-water immersion can
trigger a **cold-shock response** — gasping, rapid breathing, increased heart
rate, and blood-pressure changes.[^cold-water] The bathroom conclusion is modest:

- A cool cloth on the face: often helpful.
- An ice bath or competitive face-plunge: a different physiological experiment
  and a poor improvisation when dizzy.
- Cold tile under the palms: information, not treatment.
- If cooling feels worse, stop. The body is allowed to vote.

## Some Arousal Can Help — Too Much Jams the Controls

Don't demand zero stress before taking action. The useful range is somewhere in
the middle: enough engagement to care, not so much that everything jams.

- Reduce arousal enough for the task at hand.
- Simplify the task when arousal can't be reduced quickly.
- Use external checklists for complex decisions.

This is a broad performance principle, not a meter hidden behind your left ear.

## Comfort Inventory

Once breathing is somewhat regulated, scan your environment for comfort
resources. Check what applies:

| Resource | Available? | Details |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| Phone charger / power | ☐ | Find it. A dead phone is a dead lifeline. |
| WiFi password | ☐ | Write it here: `____________________` |
| Comics / graphic novels | ☐ | Visual stories require less cognitive load than text. Ideal for reboot mode. |
| Books | ☐ | Fiction > nonfiction right now. Escapism is a feature, not a bug. |
| Scent candle | ☐ | Olfactory stimulation directly affects the limbic system. Light it. Smell it. Neuroscience is on your side. (Skip if asthma, migraine, gas, or oxygen concerns.) |
| Water (drinking) | ☐ | Dehydration worsens anxiety. Glass of water > spiral of dread. |
| Blanket / warm thing | ☐ | Warmth signals safety to the mammalian brain. Wrap yourself like the burrito you deserve to be. |
| Safe person or pet | ☐ | Companionship without a mandatory speech. |

**Running total:** If you checked 3+, you're above the comfort threshold.
If less, the bathroom has walls and a lock — that's already 2. You're fine.

## Unhook From the Thought

Try this phrasing:

> "I notice the thought that …"

Not "the thought is false," not "I must defeat it." Place one grammatical layer
between you and the sentence. Then ask:

- What is the thought predicting?
- What fact do I know right now?
- What action fits the person I want to be for five minutes?

Example:

> "I notice the thought that everyone will hate me. The fact is that I left the
> room because I was overloaded. My next action is to text one person and say I
> need ten minutes."

Adjectives are cheaper than prophecy.[^affect-labeling]

## The Five-Minute Values Bridge

When the immediate alarm has reduced slightly:

1. Name one value: safety, honesty, kindness, dignity, rest, responsibility.
2. Choose one action under five minutes that expresses it.
3. Do not require the action to solve the whole situation.

Values are directions, not performance scores.

## The Bathroom Control Panel

Check the boring variables:

| Variable | Tiny correction |
|---|---|
| Temperature | loosen clothing; cool or warm the room safely |
| Water | take a normal drink if thirsty and swallowing is safe |
| Food | eat something familiar if you have not eaten and can do so safely |
| Noise/light | reduce one source |
| Clothing | loosen anything tight; add a warm layer if cold |
| Medication | take only as prescribed; do not improvise doses |
| Contact | tell one person what is happening |
| Information | stop searching symptoms for ten minutes |
| Exit | identify where you will go next |

## Eventually: Leaving the Bathroom

You can't live in here. (You could try, but the logistics deteriorate after
hour 3.) At some point, you'll need to re-enter the world. Here's how to make
that transition survivable.

### Conversation Strategies

1. **The deflection:** "Just needed a minute." (True, complete, and nobody's
   business.)
2. **The redirect:** Ask them a question. People love talking about themselves.
   Your problem is now their monologue.
3. **The honest-lite:** "I'm a bit overwhelmed, but I'm okay." (Vulnerable
   enough to be real, bounded enough to be safe.)
4. **The boundary:** "I can't discuss this right now. I can talk tomorrow."
5. **The exit:** "I'm done for today. I'm going home."

All five are complete sentences. None require a PowerPoint.

### The Option of Leaving

Leaving a social situation is allowed. You are not trapped. You can say: "I'm
going to head out" or "I think I'm done for today" or simply leave. "Irish
goodbye" is a time-honored tradition with a surprisingly low regret rate.

### Seeking Help: How to Ask

Asking for help is a skill, not a character trait. Script:

> "Hey, I'm going through something right now. I don't need advice — I just need
> [someone to listen / a distraction / to not be alone for a bit]. Can you
> [specific request]?"

The specificity is key. "I need help" is hard to respond to. "Can you sit with
me for 10 minutes" is easy.

### Being Yourself Is Allowed

You are not required to perform okay-ness. Awkwardness is a universal human
experience. The person you're worried about judging you? They once locked
themselves in a bathroom too. Everyone has. It's the great unspoken universal.
Welcome to the club.

### Smalltalk Toolkit

Smalltalk is a skill, not a personality trait. It can be learned. Here are three
low-effort conversation fuel sources:

1. **This guide.** "I was just reading this emergency guide in the bathroom —
   yes, really — and it turns out there's a whole flowchart for anxiety."
   Conversation started. You're welcome.
2. **Food.** Everyone eats, everyone has opinions.
3. **Media.** "Have you seen anything good lately?" is a question that works in
   every timezone.

**Short conversation loop:** Listen → Reflect → Respond. Don't prepare your
closing argument while the other person is still describing their cat.

### Nice Places / Activities After the Bathroom

Choose low-demand, reversible actions:

- **Art-consuming:** Coffee-table books exist for exactly this purpose. Pick one
  up. Have opinions about it. "That's pretty" and "what even is that" are both
  valid critical frameworks.
- **Art-making:** If there are drawing materials, a notebook, or even a pen and
  a receipt — make something. It doesn't need to be good. It needs to be
  physical evidence that you exist and made a choice.
- **Comfy places:** Find the best chair, the softest cushion, the warmest
  corner. Claim it. You've earned spatial comfort.
- Sit near an open window when safe.
- Make tea or a simple snack.
- Listen to one familiar track.
- Ask someone to sit nearby without conversation.

The activity does not need to become a hobby, identity, or monetized content
stream. It may simply occupy seven minutes without making things worse.

## A Calm Plan for Another Person

When supporting someone else:

1. Ask whether they're safe and whether medical danger may exist.
2. Reduce noise, audience, and unnecessary questions.
3. Offer choices: sit here or there, water or no water, silence or company.
4. Ask before touching.
5. Do not force breathing exercises.
6. Help make one call.
7. Stay within your own limits and involve professionals.

The goal is not to become their entire nervous system. That position has poor
hours and no pension.

## When Calm Is Not Enough

Seek professional help when:

- episodes recur or become more intense
- avoidance is shrinking daily life
- sleep, eating, work, care, or hygiene is deteriorating
- substances are becoming the main coping tool
- trauma symptoms persist
- you're frightened of what you might do
- grounding and breathing make things worse rather than better
- physical symptoms need assessment

Use **116 117** for urgent non-life-threatening medical help, **116 123** for
crisis conversation, a local social psychiatric service, or a psychiatric
emergency department. Use **112** for acute self-harm, other-endangerment,
severe confusion, or medical danger.

```
    │                                       You are not wasting their time.
    │
    └─ It can wait, but it shouldn't wait forever → Make an appointment
                                                    this week. Not next
                                                    week. This week.
                                                    → 📍 Professional Support
                                                      Directory (Ch.7)
```

**How to reach out (scripts for different situations):**

1. **To a friend:** "Hey, I've been going through some stuff. Can we talk? No
   rush, but soon would be good."
2. **To a professional:** "I'd like to make an appointment. I'm dealing with
   [anxiety / a difficult situation / something I'd like to talk through]."
   You don't need the perfect words — they've heard worse.
3. **To a crisis line:** Just call. They'll guide the conversation. You
   literally just need to dial.
