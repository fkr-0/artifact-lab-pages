# Body Owner's Manual — Your Body Is Talking. Let's Figure Out What It's Saying.

Your body has been sending you signals your entire life. Most of the time you
ignore them successfully. Right now, in this bathroom, you can't — because the
signals are loud, confusing, and your anxiety is providing unhelpful real-time
commentary.

This chapter is a translation guide. Body signal on the left, likely meaning on
the right. Not a substitute for a doctor — but a substitute for the catastrophic
interpretation your brain is currently running unsupervised.

---

## 1. Understanding Body Signals

Your nervous system has two modes that matter here: the sympathetic ("something
is happening, deploy everything") and the parasympathetic ("stand down, digest
lunch, exist calmly"). Under stress, the sympathetic system runs the show. The
problem is that its alarm signals feel identical whether the threat is a bear or
a difficult conversation you're avoiding.[^porges-polyvagal]

### The Signal Translation Table

**Your body is producing symptoms. Your anxiety is producing interpretations. They
are not the same department.**

| Signal | What anxiety says | What it probably is | When to actually worry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Racing heart | "Heart attack. Definitely." | Adrenaline doing its job. HR 100-130 bpm under acute stress is normal.[^heart-rate-stress] | Chest pain radiating to arm/jaw, irregular rhythm, doesn't slow with breathing exercises after 20 min |
| Shallow breathing | "Can't breathe. Lungs failing." | Hyperventilation from stress. You're getting *too much* oxygen, not too little. | Lips/fingers turning blue, wheezing, unable to speak a full sentence |
| Tingling in hands/face | "Stroke. This is a stroke." | CO2 drop from hyperventilation. Classic and harmless. | One-sided weakness, facial droop, slurred speech, sudden severe headache |
| Tight chest | "See: heart attack, above." | Intercostal muscle tension. You've been clenching your torso like a fist. | Crushing pressure that worsens with exertion, not relieved by position change |
| Sweating | "Something is seriously wrong." | Thermoregulation under sympathetic activation. Your body is cooling an engine that revved too high. | Cold sweats with chest pain, confusion, or loss of consciousness |
| Trembling/shaking | "I'm losing control of my body." | Adrenaline discharge. Your muscles are burning off unused fight-or-flight fuel.[^tremor-physiology] | New-onset tremor that persists after calming, affects one side only |
| Nausea | "Going to be sick. In public." | Blood redirected from gut to muscles. Digestion got deprioritised. | Vomiting blood, severe abdominal pain, nausea after head injury |
| Dizziness | "About to faint. Or die." | Blood pressure shift + hyperventilation. Sit down. | Loss of consciousness, spinning that doesn't stop, sudden onset with worst headache of your life |
| Feeling unreal | "I'm going insane." | Depersonalisation/derealisation. A protective dissociative response, not psychosis.[^depersonalisation] | Persistent for days, accompanied by hallucinations or inability to recognise surroundings |
| Dry mouth | "Something toxic is happening." | Sympathetic activation reduces saliva production. Standard issue. | Accompanied by difficulty swallowing, swollen tongue, or allergic reaction signs |

### The 60-Second Body Scan

Not the meditation kind. The diagnostic kind.

1. **Heart.** Place two fingers on the inside of your wrist. Count beats for 15
   seconds, multiply by 4. Write the number down. You now have data instead of
   dread.

2. **Breathing.** Are you breathing from your chest or your belly? Chest
   breathing = sympathetic activation. Place a hand on your stomach. If it's not
   moving, your diaphragm has clocked out. Invite it back.

3. **Muscles.** Scan jaw, shoulders, fists, thighs. Anything clenched? Unclench
   it. It will clench again in 30 seconds. Unclench it again. This is the game.
   You will play it many rounds.

4. **Temperature.** Are you cold or hot? Cold extremities with a warm core =
   blood has centralised (stress response). Hot all over = adrenaline flush.
   Neither is dangerous, but knowing which one explains why your hands feel
   weird.

5. **Gut.** Churning? Cramping? Empty? We'll get to this in detail shortly,
   because you're in a bathroom and your GI tract has opinions.

## 2. The Gastrointestinal Chapter You Didn't Ask For

You're reading an emergency guide in a bathroom. Let's talk about why your
stomach is doing that.

### Why Stress Hits the Gut

Your gastrointestinal tract contains roughly 500 million neurons — the enteric
nervous system, sometimes called the "second brain." It's connected to your
central nervous system via the vagus nerve, which means your gut and your brain
are in constant bidirectional conversation.[^gut-brain-axis] When your brain
panics, your gut gets the memo.

The stress response does several things to digestion simultaneously:

- **Slows gastric emptying** (food sits like a brick)
- **Increases colonic motility** (the other end speeds up — urgently)
- **Reduces blood flow to the GI tract** (digestion deprioritised)
- **Alters gut microbiome signalling** (longer-term, but relevant if you've been
  stressed for days)

The result: nausea from above, urgency from below, and cramps everywhere in
between. Your gut is not broken. It's responding to a situation your brain has
classified as a survival event, even if the actual threat is "someone asked me a
question I didn't know the answer to."

### IBS Flare-Ups Under Stress

If you have irritable bowel syndrome — diagnosed or suspected — stress is your
most reliable trigger. The ROME IV criteria define IBS as recurrent abdominal
pain averaging at least one day per week in the last three months, associated
with defecation, change in stool frequency, or change in stool form.[^rome-iv]

You don't need to self-diagnose right now. But if this pattern is familiar — if
stress reliably sends you to the bathroom with cramping, urgency, or altered
stool — you're not imagining it, and you're not alone. IBS affects 5-10% of the
global population.[^ibs-prevalence]

### Position and Breathing for GI Distress

What to do while you're here:

1. **Lean forward slightly.** Elbows on knees, gentle forward tilt. This
   reduces abdominal pressure and is biomechanically friendlier for your
   pelvic floor.

2. **Breathe into your belly, not your chest.** Diaphragmatic breathing
   stimulates the vagus nerve, which nudges the parasympathetic system —
   the one that actually runs digestion — back online.

3. **Don't strain.** Straining increases intra-abdominal pressure, can trigger
   a vasovagal response (sudden blood pressure drop, dizziness, possible
   fainting), and doesn't actually help. If nothing is happening, nothing is
   happening. Wait or walk.

4. **Gentle abdominal massage.** Clockwise circles following the path of the
   colon (right side up, across the top, left side down). Light pressure only.
   This can help move trapped gas and reduce cramping.[^abdominal-massage]

5. **Warm water.** If you have access to warm (not hot) water, sipping it can
   help relax smooth muscle in the GI tract.

### Stress vs. Something Else: A Quick Check

| Feature | Stress-related GI distress | Seek help |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Correlated with stressor | Sudden, no obvious trigger |
| Pain location | Diffuse, crampy, moves around | Localised, severe, getting worse |
| Blood in stool | No | Yes — any amount. Not negotiable. |
| Fever | No | Yes |
| Pattern | Familiar, you've had this before | New, different from your usual |
| Duration | Resolves as stress reduces | Persists or worsens over hours |
| Vomiting | Possible, mild | Persistent, contains blood or bile, or can't keep water down |

If the right column applies: this isn't stress management territory. Use the
Self Ambulance Guide or call for help.

### Nausea Management

Nausea under stress is common and usually not dangerous. To manage it:

- **Don't fight it.** Resisting nausea increases tension, which increases
  nausea. Acknowledge it exists.
- **Sip cold water** in small amounts. Not large gulps.
- **Cool cloth on the back of the neck.** The vagal connection again.
- **Peppermint** if available (gum, tea, even toothpaste in a pinch). Menthol
  relaxes the lower oesophageal sphincter and smooth muscle.[^peppermint-gi]
- **Controlled breathing.** Slow exhales. The same technique that calms panic
  also calms the vomiting centre — they share neural real estate.
- **Pressure point.** Press firmly on the inside of your wrist, about three
  finger-widths from the crease (P6/Nei Guan point). Evidence is mixed but
  risk is zero.[^acupressure-nausea]

## 3. Pain Assessment Without Panic

Pain is information. Anxiety is commentary. Let's separate them.

### The Numeric Rating Scale (NRS)

The NRS is what hospitals use. It works here too.[^nrs-pain]

Rate your pain from 0 to 10:

| Score | What it means | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | No pain | You're reading this proactively. Good for you. |
| 1-3 | Mild. Noticeable but not limiting. | Monitor. Note location and type. Continue with what you were doing. |
| 4-6 | Moderate. Distracting. Affects function. | Sit down if standing. Track changes over 10-15 minutes. Consider simple analgesia if available and appropriate. |
| 7-8 | Severe. Difficult to think about anything else. | Stop what you're doing. Position for comfort. Consider calling someone. |
| 9-10 | Worst imaginable. | This isn't a self-help moment. Call for help. 112 if warranted. |

### Pain Characteristics Checklist

Where it is matters less than how it behaves. Track these:

- **Location.** Point to it. One finger or a whole hand? Pinpoint pain and
  diffuse pain mean different things.
- **Quality.** Sharp? Dull? Burning? Cramping? Pressure? Throbbing? Each word
  narrows the differential.
- **Timing.** When did it start? Sudden or gradual? Constant or comes and goes?
  Getting better, worse, or stable?
- **Movement.** Does it radiate (spread) anywhere? Pain that radiates follows
  nerve paths and tells a story.
- **Provocation.** What makes it worse? Breathing? Moving? Pressing on it?
  Eating? Nothing?
- **Relief.** What makes it better? Position change? Pressure? Stillness?

Write this down. If you end up calling for help, you'll hand someone a clinical
history instead of "it hurts and I'm scared." The first version helps them help
you faster.

### Red Flags: Call Someone Now

These are not "monitor and see" situations:

- **Chest pain** that radiates to the left arm, jaw, or back, especially with
  shortness of breath, sweating, or nausea
- **Sudden severe headache** — "worst headache of my life" — especially if
  you've never had it before
- **Abdominal pain** that is rigid, board-like on palpation, or worsening
  rapidly
- **Pain after injury** to the head, neck, or spine
- **Sudden severe pain anywhere** in someone on blood thinners
- **One-sided weakness or numbness** with or without pain — time-critical
  stroke indicator (FAST: Face, Arms, Speech, Time)
- **Pain with high fever** (above 39C / 102F)
- **Testicular pain** — sudden onset. Not embarrassing. Time-sensitive.

If any of these apply: do not finish this chapter. Use the Self Ambulance Guide.
Call 112 if in doubt. Nobody has ever been criticised for calling too early.

### Pain That Anxiety Amplifies

Some pain is real but amplified. Tension headaches, jaw pain from clenching,
chest tightness from muscle tension, stomach cramps from stress — all genuinely
hurt, and all get louder when you're anxious about them.

The test: if the pain decreases when you change position, relax the surrounding
muscles, or get distracted for a moment — it's likely tension-related. That
doesn't mean it's fake. It means the treatment is relaxation, not an ambulance.

## 4. Hydration and Blood Sugar

Two of the most common reasons people feel terrible in bathrooms, and two of the
easiest to fix. You have a tap. Let's use it.

### Dehydration

Your body is roughly 60% water. Drop that by 1-2% and your brain notices before
your mouth does.[^dehydration]

| Sign | Severity | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Thirst, dry mouth | Mild | Drink water. That's it. That's the intervention. |
| Dark yellow urine | Mild-moderate | You're in a bathroom. You can check this. Drink more water. |
| Headache, fatigue | Moderate | 500ml of water over 30 minutes. Not all at once. |
| Dizziness on standing | Moderate | Drink water, add a pinch of salt if available. Stand slowly. |
| Rapid heartbeat | Moderate-severe | Sit down, drink water steadily, monitor. If it doesn't improve in 20 min, seek help. |
| Confusion, very dry skin, no urine output | Severe | Medical attention needed. This is beyond tap-water territory. |

**Note:** Dehydration mimics anxiety almost perfectly — rapid heart rate,
dizziness, difficulty concentrating, fatigue, headache. Before concluding you're
having a mental health crisis, drink a glass of water and wait 15 minutes.
Sometimes the existential dread was just thirst.

### Low Blood Sugar (Hypoglycaemia)

If you haven't eaten in several hours, or you've been running on caffeine and
adrenaline, your blood sugar may be low. Non-diabetic hypoglycaemia is less
dangerous than the diabetic version but feels equally awful.[^hypoglycemia]

Signs:

- Trembling, shakiness (easily confused with anxiety tremor)
- Sweating (easily confused with stress sweating)
- Irritability, difficulty concentrating
- Lightheadedness
- Hunger (sometimes masked by nausea)
- Feeling "wrong" in a way you can't articulate

**The test:** When did you last eat? If it's been more than 4-5 hours, low blood
sugar is a likely contributor to how you feel.

**The fix:**

1. Eat something with simple sugars first — a sweet drink, fruit juice, sugar
   packet, sweets if available. This raises blood sugar within 10-15 minutes.
2. Follow with something more complex — a biscuit, bread, a banana — to sustain
   the level.
3. If you have nothing: the tap provides water but not glucose. Ask someone to
   bring you something to eat. This is not a dramatic request.

### When Trembling Is Metabolic vs. Anxiety

Both low blood sugar and anxiety cause trembling. Here's how to tell them apart:

| Feature | Metabolic (blood sugar) | Anxiety/adrenaline |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Gradual, correlates with fasting | Sudden, correlates with stressor |
| Eating helps | Yes, noticeably within 15 min | Marginally or not at all |
| Accompaniments | Hunger, sweating, irritability | Racing thoughts, chest tightness |
| Mental state | Foggy, can't concentrate | Hyper-alert, can't stop concentrating |
| Resolution | Eat something, it goes away | Calming techniques, time |

If in doubt: eat something AND do a breathing exercise. Cover both bases. The
worst outcome is a slightly unnecessary biscuit.

## 5. Medication and Substances

Your body doesn't process chemicals in isolation. If you've taken something,
missed something, or consumed something recreational, that context changes
everything about how to read your body's signals.

### Did You Take Something?

Common medications with side effects that mimic emergencies:

| Medication | Can cause | Looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Beta-blockers (propranolol, atenolol) | Dizziness, fatigue, cold extremities, low HR | "Something is very wrong" |
| SSRIs/SNRIs (sertraline, venlafaxine) | Nausea, jitteriness, sweating — especially early in treatment or after dose change[^ssri-side-effects] | Anxiety attack, GI emergency |
| Stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamines) | Rapid heart rate, tremor, anxiety, decreased appetite | Panic attack, cardiac event |
| Antihistamines (diphenhydramine, cetirizine) | Drowsiness, dry mouth, confusion | "Something is wrong with my brain" |
| Decongestants (pseudoephedrine) | Rapid heart rate, anxiety, tremor, insomnia | Panic attack |
| Corticosteroids (prednisone) | Mood changes, insomnia, increased appetite, GI upset | Mood disorder, GI problem |
| Antibiotics | Nausea, diarrhoea, abdominal pain | GI emergency |
| Pain relievers (NSAIDs) | Stomach pain, nausea on empty stomach | Acute abdominal problem |

### Did You Miss Something?

Missed doses can be as symptomatic as new doses:

- **Antidepressants** (especially venlafaxine, paroxetine): Discontinuation
  syndrome — "brain zaps," dizziness, nausea, irritability, flu-like
  feelings.[^discontinuation] This is not a new illness. It's withdrawal.
  Take your dose if you have it with you.

- **Blood pressure medication:** Rebound hypertension can cause headache,
  anxiety, palpitations. Not dangerous in the short term but unpleasant.

- **Thyroid medication:** Missed doses accumulate. One day won't cause a crisis,
  but a week might explain some symptoms.

- **Birth control:** Missing a dose doesn't cause acute symptoms, but the
  anxiety about missing it can produce plenty.

- **Psychiatric medication:** If you've missed antipsychotics, mood stabilisers,
  or benzodiazepines, some of what you're experiencing may be withdrawal or
  rebound. Take your dose. If you can't, note the timing for when you speak to
  your prescriber.

### Caffeine

The most popular psychoactive substance on earth, and nobody treats it with
appropriate respect.[^caffeine-effects]

- **Too much:** Anxiety, rapid heart rate, tremor, GI upset, insomnia. Caffeine
  has a half-life of 5-6 hours. That 4pm espresso is still 50% active at 10pm.
- **Withdrawal:** Headache, fatigue, irritability, difficulty concentrating,
  depressed mood. Onset 12-24 hours after last dose, peaks at 20-51 hours.
- **With anxiety:** Caffeine directly antagonises adenosine receptors, which
  increases norepinephrine release. Translation: it makes your anxiety
  hardware more sensitive.

If you've had more than your usual amount: wait it out, hydrate, eat something.
If you've had less than your usual amount: the headache and fatigue are
withdrawal, not disease.

### Alcohol

Relevant because alcohol and bathrooms frequently co-occur.

- **Acute effects:** Disinhibition, impaired judgement, dehydration, GI
  irritation, vasodilation (warm face, cold hands).
- **The morning after:** Hangover symptoms overlap heavily with anxiety
  symptoms — nausea, headache, tremor, racing heart, dread. "Hangxiety" is
  real: alcohol disrupts GABA/glutamate balance, and the rebound the next day
  pushes your nervous system toward excitation.[^hangover-anxiety]
- **Interaction with medication:** Alcohol + antidepressants, alcohol +
  benzodiazepines, alcohol + antihistamines = amplified sedation, impaired
  coordination, respiratory depression in extreme cases. If you're on
  medication and you've been drinking, note both for whoever you talk to next.

### Cannabis, Nicotine, and Other Substances

Brief notes because comprehensiveness matters:

- **Cannabis:** Can cause tachycardia (rapid heart rate), anxiety, paranoia,
  nausea, and dizziness. These are known effects, not emergencies — unless
  they're new, severe, or accompanied by chest pain.
- **Nicotine:** Acute effects include increased heart rate and blood pressure.
  Withdrawal includes irritability, anxiety, difficulty concentrating. Both
  mimic bigger problems.
- **Anything else:** If you've taken something recreational and you're not sure
  what it was, or you're experiencing symptoms that feel different from
  previous use — that's a "tell someone" situation, not a "read a guide"
  situation. Call a friend. Call a crisis line. Honesty is more useful than
  pride when pharmacology is involved.

## 6. Recovery Position and Safety

If you feel faint, lightheaded, or like consciousness is becoming optional —
bathrooms are small rooms with hard surfaces. The priority is: do not fall and
hit something.

### Signs You Might Faint

- Vision narrowing or greying out
- Sound becoming distant or muffled
- Legs feeling weak or rubbery
- Sudden warmth and sweating followed by cold and clammy
- Nausea intensifying rapidly

These are pre-syncope signs. You have seconds to minutes. Use them wisely.

### What to Do: The Bathroom Safety Sequence

**Step 1: Get low immediately.**

Do not try to walk to a better location. Where you are is where you're staying.

- If you're standing: sit on the floor. Not on the toilet, not on the edge of
  the bathtub. The floor. It can't drop you any further.
- If you're sitting: slide forward off the seat to the floor. Controlled
  descent. Dignity is secondary to skull integrity.

**Step 2: Protect your head.**

- If you're on the floor, lie on your side (recovery position).
- Grab a towel, bathmat, folded clothing — anything between your head and tile.
- If nothing is available, your arm under your head is better than nothing.

**Step 3: Elevate your legs if possible.**

Rest your feet on the toilet, bathtub edge, or against the wall. This helps
blood return to your brain. It doesn't need to be dramatic — even a slight
elevation helps.[^syncope-management]

**Step 4: Stay down.**

Do not jump up the moment you feel better. Lie still for at least 2-3 minutes
after the faint feeling passes. Then:

1. Sit up slowly. Wait 30 seconds.
2. If okay: move to kneeling. Wait 30 seconds.
3. If still okay: stand, holding something stable. Wait 30 seconds.
4. If the dizziness returns at any stage: go back one step and wait longer.

### The Modified Recovery Position (Bathroom Edition)

The standard recovery position assumes a clear floor. Bathrooms offer
approximately 1.5 square metres of obstacle course. Adapt:

```
Standard recovery position — adapted for small spaces:

    ┌──────────────────────┐
    │                      │
    │   Towel/mat under    │
    │   head               │
    │      ___             │
    │     / . \  ←  Head   │
    │    │     │    on     │
    │     \___/    soft    │
    │       │      surface │
    │   ┌───┘              │
    │   │ ← Top arm        │
    │   │    forward       │
    │   ├───┐              │
    │   │   │ ← Top knee   │
    │   │   │    bent      │
    │   │   │    forward   │
    │   │                  │
    │   └── Feet can       │
    │       rest against   │
    │       wall/tub       │
    └──────────────────────┘

Key points:
  - Lie on your side, not your back
  - Top knee bent forward for stability
  - Top arm in front to prevent rolling
  - Something soft under head
  - Mouth angled toward floor (if vomiting)
```

### Vasovagal Syncope: The Bathroom Special

Vasovagal syncope — fainting triggered by the vagus nerve — is the most common
cause of fainting, and bathrooms are its favourite venue.[^vasovagal] Common
triggers in this setting:

- Straining during a bowel movement
- Standing up quickly from a seated position
- Heat (hot bath, shower, small enclosed room)
- Pain
- Seeing blood
- Dehydration
- Prolonged standing

If you've fainted and regained consciousness:

1. Stay down. Seriously. The floor is your friend now.
2. Drink water when you can.
3. Note the time. If consciousness was lost for more than a few seconds, or if
   you hit your head, tell someone — even if you feel fine now.
4. Do not lock the door after a faint. Someone may need to reach you.

### When Fainting Is Not "Just" Vasovagal

Seek medical attention if:

- You fainted during exercise
- You fainted while lying down (not from standing)
- You have chest pain, palpitations, or shortness of breath
- There was no warning (sudden, out of nowhere)
- You've fainted multiple times recently
- You hit your head and can't clearly remember what happened
- You have a cardiac history or are on heart medication
- Recovery takes more than a few minutes

One vasovagal faint in a hot bathroom after skipping breakfast is annoying. A
pattern of unexplained syncope needs investigation.

## Quick Reference Card

For when you need the summary, not the chapter.

| Symptom | Most likely cause | First action |
|---|---|---|
| Racing heart | Adrenaline / caffeine / dehydration | Sit, breathe, hydrate, wait 15 min |
| Trembling | Adrenaline discharge / low blood sugar | Eat something, breathe, wait |
| Nausea | Stress gut response / dehydration | Small sips, cool cloth on neck, lean forward |
| Dizziness | Hyperventilation / dehydration / standing too fast | Sit or lie down, drink water, breathe slowly |
| GI urgency | Stress-induced colonic motility | Lean forward, breathe, don't strain |
| Chest tightness | Muscle tension / hyperventilation | Change position, unclench torso, slow exhale |
| Tingling | Hyperventilation (CO2 drop) | Slow your breathing, especially the exhale |
| Feeling faint | Vasovagal / dehydration / blood sugar | Get to the floor NOW, then figure out why |

### The Two Questions That Matter

Before you spiral further:

1. **Have I eaten and had water in the last 4 hours?** If no: fix that first.
   An alarming number of emergencies are just a sandwich.

2. **Is this symptom new, worsening, or accompanied by a red flag?** If no:
   manage it here. If yes: call someone. The Self Ambulance Guide exists for
   exactly this.

*Your body is not your enemy. It's a reporting system with limited vocabulary
and a flair for the dramatic. Learn to read it, and most of what it says is:
"I need water, food, rest, and for you to stop clenching everything." Give it
those things. See what remains. Handle that.*
